        National Railways

        Lay track between real cities, run your trains, and get every passenger where they are going before a station overflows. Own all of your home state (California, unless you pick another in Settings) and the states next to it open.

        How to play
        • Build: drag from one city to another to lay track. Keep dragging to add stops.
        • Extend: drag the tab at either end of a line. Drag an end to the line's other end (3 stations or more) to close it into a loop: trains then go round and round.
        • Grow: you start with 3 counties around Los Angeles. Tap a county with a dashed orange outline to unlock it with money, or deliver passengers next to it to earn it for free. Towns appear inside counties you own.
        • Cargo: farms send produce, ships bring containers to ports (anchor), and desert and mountain towns send trucks. Cargo waits in a yard left of the station and needs its own train: Freight, Dock train or Truck bypass. Cargo never ends your game, but it pays well.
        • Day and night: fewer people travel at night, when Night trains run. Tourists (★) head for scenic places, most at weekends. Flights land at airports (✈) with a burst of travelers.
        • Earthquakes and wildfires: from week 10 they damage track (red ✕). Trains turn back at the damage. A Work train on that line repairs it (you get one free when they begin, and a Work train waiting in your depot goes to the damage by itself); otherwise it mends after 3 days.
        • Cities: Los Angeles, San Francisco, San Diego, Sacramento and San Jose have their own city maps (M badge). Tap the station and choose Open city map, then build Tram, Metro or (in San Francisco) Cable car lines between neighborhoods. Riders cross town or come to the main station to catch a train, and train passengers ride home. Neighborhoods can overflow too, but only on the map you're looking at (the others keep running safely, half full at most), and an overwhelmed city doesn't end the game: a head of transportation takes it over, for a fee, and its lines are frozen while they run it. Each city has its own budget (it opens with 200k from its state, and the state pays when it runs short), and you can hire a head of transportation: after 3 weeks of settling in (you still run the city), they run it safely for 100 weeks, but its lines are frozen and management keeps 90% of its profit.
        • Goals: tap the clipboard to see contracts (optional jobs with a reward and a deadline) and, in Levels mode, the checklist for the current level.
        • Stations: tap a station to upgrade it: an Interchange (FB150k) holds twice the riders and cargo, a Grand terminal (FB400k more) three times, and people change trains faster there.
        • Emergency links: tap a station to join it to a station up to 60 miles away. It costs 20 times normal track but needs no free line, and runs its own shuttle.
        • Zoom: pinch or scroll to zoom, drag empty map to move around.
        • Lines: tap a county you own (or Lines at the bottom) to see that state's lines: its own (bright colors), national lines that cross a state border (deep colors) and international lines that cross into Canada (pastel, twice as thick). Each kind has its own count; tap + to buy one more. Tap a line there to add trains, upgrade to high-speed track, or delete it.
        • Geography: the map shows real mountains, lakes and rivers. Track over a mountain pass (like Donner Pass) costs more and trains slow down on the climb; elsewhere mountains need an expensive tunnel, and some are too wide to tunnel. Bays and lakes need a bridge: cheaper at real bridges like the Golden Gate. Rivers need small bridges. Choose Gentle or Tough difficulty under Settings.
        • Money: track and trains cost money. Passengers pay a fare when they arrive, and trains cost a little to run each week.
        • Budgets and states: every state has its own budget (tap the money at the bottom). Fares for trips inside a state go to its budget (less a 10% tax to national money; cities pay 10% of their fares to their state), and the state pays for its own track, trains and counties. Across a state border the states share: track by how much of it is on each one's land, and each fare by the miles the trip rode on each one's land. National money gets the taxes, contract rewards and grants, and covers any state that runs short. Money can't be moved between budgets by hand. Once you own all of California, Nevada, Arizona and Oregon open. A state can hire a head of transportation too, just like a city.
